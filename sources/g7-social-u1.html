A. The origin of the Earth

Start-up questions
  • What do you already know about how the Earth and the solar system began?
  • Where did the Earth come from, and how did it form?

The Earth's beginning is tied to the beginning of the whole solar system. Many scientists think the Sun, the planets and the smaller bodies all formed from one huge cloud of hot, swirling gas and dust. This cloud was mostly hydrogen and helium.

Slowly, gravity pulled the gas and dust together into a thin, spinning disc. Over time the disc split into rings that kept spinning. The rings at the centre formed the Sun. The outer rings formed the eight planets, including Earth. Geologists estimate the Earth formed about 4.6 billion years ago.

Correction: the printed book says "a large flammable of hot swirling gases". The intended word is cloud (scientists call it a nebula).

FocusUntil August 2006 Pluto was counted as a planet. That year the International Astronomical Union decided that Pluto, Ceres and Eris (first called 2003 UB313) should be called dwarf planets.

B. Members of the solar system

Our solar system is made of one star, eight planets and countless smaller bodies such as dwarf planets, asteroids and meteoroids.

I. The Sun

The Sun is the centre of the solar system. It is huge and made of extremely hot gases. Its pull holds the whole system together, and it is the main source of heat and light. We do not feel its full heat because, although it is our nearest star, it is about 150 million km away.

FocusThe Sun is a star, just like the stars you see at night. It is the largest object in the solar system and holds 99.8% of the system's mass.

II. Planets

Start-up questions
  • What is a planet?
  • What is the name of the planet we live on?

The word "planet" comes from the Greek word planetai, which means "wanderers". There are eight planets. All of them travel around the Sun on paths called orbits, which are oval (elliptical) in shape. By their distance from the Sun they form two groups.

Inner planets. Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars are closest to the Sun. They are solid, with rocky surfaces, so astronomers call them the terrestrial planets. They are small compared with the outer planets.

Outer planets. Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune are farthest from the Sun. They are much larger and made mostly of gases, so they are called gas giants.

A. The shape of the earth

Start-up questions
  • What is the shape of the Earth?
  • How can we prove the shape of the Earth?
  • What evidence shows the Earth is round?

Earth is one of the eight planets. Its shape is not perfectly round: it is slightly wider at the Equator. This shape is called an oblate spheroid. The Earth's spinning causes it: the Earth bulges in the middle and is slightly flattened at the North and South Poles.

Check your understanding

What does "oblate spheroid" mean?

Evidence of the Earth's shape

If the Earth were a flat disc, the rising Sun would be seen everywhere at the same moment. This does not happen: places in the east see sunrise first. And when a ship sails toward land, its mast appears before its body. On a flat Earth the whole ship would appear at once. This shows the Earth is not flat. Precise measurements show it is an oblate spheroid; scientists describe the exact, slightly lumpy shape as a geoid.

B. Movements of the earth

Start-up questions
  • How does the Earth move?
  • What is the difference between rotation and revolution?
  • How do rotation and revolution affect days, years and seasons?
  • How long does the Earth take to rotate once?

Nothing in the universe stands still. Planets, moons and other bodies all spin and travel at different speeds. The Earth has two movements: rotation and revolution.

i. Rotation

Rotation is the Earth spinning on its axis. One rotation takes about 24 hours. The Earth spins from west to east, so the Sun appears to rise in the east and set in the west. The axis is tilted about 23½°. This tilt affects how much daylight and warmth places get, and the weather from season to season.

Correction: the printed book says the axis "is perpendicular to the Earth's orbit". The axis is actually tilted 23½° away from the perpendicular.

Check your understanding

Why does the Sun seem to rise in the east?

Effects of rotation

1. Day and night. As the Earth spins, half faces the Sun and has day while the other half faces away and has night. This gives a daily cycle of light and darkness, temperature and humidity.

2. High and low tides. Tides are the regular rise and fall of the sea. They are caused mainly by the Moon's pull, and partly by the Sun's. As the Earth rotates, places pass through these pulls and get high and low tides.

Check your understanding

What mainly causes tides in the sea?

ii. Revolution of the earth

Revolution is the Earth's journey around the Sun on its orbit. One revolution takes 365¼ days, or one year. The Earth moves at about 107,000 km per hour (about 67,000 miles per hour).

Check your understanding

How long does one revolution of the Earth take?

Effects of revolution

a. Day and night change length. Because the axis is tilted, the Sun seems to move north and south of the Equator during the year. On about March 21 and September 23 the Sun is directly over the Equator, and day and night are equal everywhere. These days are equinoxes. On about June 21 the Sun is over the Tropic of Cancer: the longest day in the Northern Hemisphere and the longest night in the Southern. On about December 21 the Sun is over the Tropic of Capricorn: the longest day in the Southern Hemisphere and the longest night in the Northern. These days are solstices.

b. Seasons. Revolution together with the tilted axis causes four seasons: summer (Kiremt), autumn (Meher), winter (Bega) and spring (Belg or Tsedey).

Activity 1.2

Individual work

1. How does the Earth's rotation cause day and night?
As the Earth spins, the side facing the Sun has day and the side facing away has night. Each place moves from day into night and back every 24 hours.
2. What effect does the tilt of the Earth's axis have?
The tilt makes each hemisphere lean toward the Sun for part of the year and away for the other part. This causes the seasons and changes how long days and nights are.
3. Why isn't there always 12 hours of day and 12 hours of night everywhere?
Because the axis is tilted while the Earth travels around the Sun. Only at the equinoxes are day and night equal everywhere. At other times the hemisphere leaning toward the Sun has longer days.

1.3.1 External components of the earth

Start-up questions
  • Do you know what the lithosphere, hydrosphere, biosphere and atmosphere are?
  • Do these components interact with each other?

The Earth's physical environment has four components, or subsystems: the lithosphere (land), the hydrosphere (water), the biosphere (living things) and the atmosphere (air).

Word originThese names come from Greek: lithos = stone, hydro = water, bio = life, atmos = vapour.

A. The lithosphere

The lithosphere is the solid, rocky part of the Earth. It is the coolest and most rigid part. Its surface is very uneven, with mountains, plateaus, plains and valleys. In Ethiopia it reaches from Ras Dejen (Ras Dashen), the highest peak, down to Dallol, about 116 m below sea level.

Note: the book gives Ras Dejen's height as 4,620 m. Newer measurements give about 4,550 m.

B. The hydrosphere

The hydrosphere is all the water on Earth, as liquid, ice and water vapour, in oceans, seas, lakes and rivers. Oceans are its biggest part: they cover about 71% of the Earth's surface and hold about 97% of its water.

C. The biosphere

The biosphere is the part of the Earth where living things exist. It is a zone near the surface, reaching from the ocean floor up several kilometres into the air.

D. The atmosphere

The atmosphere is the envelope of air around the Earth, a mixture of gases. The most common gas is nitrogen (78%), which enters the air when volcanoes erupt and when dead plants and animals decay. Next is oxygen (21%), made mainly by plants. Together they are about 99% of the air. The rest is argon, carbon dioxide, methane and water vapour.

How do the Earth's spheres interact?

The four spheres are closely connected, and their interaction shapes every environment. For example, high mountains are colder than valleys: there the lithosphere meets thinner air and more precipitation, making a cool or icy climate, and plants and animals have adapted to it. Birds (biosphere) fly through the air (atmosphere). Water (hydrosphere) flows through soil (lithosphere). People breathe air, drink water and walk on the ground, so we could not live without any of the four. People build dams from rock, and use flowing water to spin turbines that make electricity.

1.3.2 Internal structure of the earth

1.3.2.1 How do we know what is inside the earth?

Start-up questions
  • List the sources of information about the inside of the Earth.
  • How do we get information about the Earth's interior?

We live on the surface of a globe with a radius of about 6,400 km, yet nobody has dug more than a few kilometres down. What we know about the inside comes from direct and indirect sources.

Direct sources (real material)Indirect sources (clues)
Mining: digging out useful materials. Gold mines in South Africa have brought up rock from 3–4 km deep.Earthquake waves: an earthquake is a natural shaking of the ground caused by a sudden release of energy. Its waves travel in all directions and reveal what the materials inside are like.
Volcanic eruptions: molten rock (magma) thrown onto the surface can be studied in laboratories.Meteorites: their materials are similar to Earth's, and many have iron and nickel cores. This suggests the Earth's core is also iron and nickel.
Deep drilling: the deepest hole ever drilled, the Kola well in Russia, reached about 12 km.

1.3.2.2 Internal structure of the earth

The inside of the Earth has three main layers: the crust, the mantle and the core.

FocusThink of the Earth like an apple: skin, flesh and core. In the Earth they are the crust, the mantle and the core.

A. Crust. The outermost and thinnest layer, made of hard rock. It has two parts. Continental crust is about 30–40 km thick; its main elements are silicon (Si) and aluminium (Al), so it is called SIAL. Oceanic crust is about 5–6 km thick; its main elements are silicon and magnesium (Mg), so it is called SIMA.

B. Mantle. The layer below the crust, about 2,900 km thick, and the thickest layer: it makes up about 84% of the Earth's volume. Its main elements are silicon and magnesium (SIMA). The mantle is mostly solid rock that can flow very slowly. In some places near its top, rock melts to form magma.

Correction: the printed book says the lower mantle is molten. The lower mantle is solid; melting happens only in limited zones near the top of the mantle. The book also writes "Silica (Si)": Si is the element silicon; silica is a compound of silicon and oxygen.

C. Core. The innermost layer, made mainly of nickel (Ni) and iron (Fe), so it is called NIFE. Its temperature is about 6,000°C and it makes up about 15% of the Earth's volume. The outer core is liquid. In the inner core the pressure is so great that the metal is squeezed solid.

1.4.2 Major minerals

Start-up question

The crust is made of rocks, and all rocks are made of minerals. A mineral is a natural, non-living solid with a fixed internal structure and a definite chemical make-up. Minerals are the building blocks of rocks. There are many minerals, but most rocks are made of a few common ones, called rock-forming minerals.

1.4.3 Major rocks forming the crust

Start-up questions
  • List the major rocks that form the crust.
  • How are different rocks formed?

By how they form, rocks are grouped into three types: igneous, sedimentary and metamorphic.

1. Igneous rock

"Igneous" comes from the Latin ignis, fire. Igneous rocks form when molten rock (magma) cools and becomes solid, either on the surface or inside the Earth. Magma that reaches the surface is called lava.

A. Extrusive igneous rocks, also called volcanic rocks, form when lava cools on the Earth's surface. Examples: basalt and obsidian.

B. Intrusive igneous rocks, also called plutonic rocks, form when magma cools and crystallises inside the Earth. Granite is the most common example.

Check your understanding

Granite cooled slowly, deep underground. What kind of rock is it?

FocusMagma is molten rock below the surface; it is called lava once it reaches the surface. It forms about 60–200 km deep, where temperatures are between 900°C and 1,600°C.

What is lava?

2. Sedimentary rock

"Sedimentary" comes from the Latin sedimentum, settling. These rocks form when sediments (mud, sand, shells) are carried by erosion and settle in one place, mostly in rivers, seas and oceans. They form in layers; each layer is the sediment laid down at one time, and the lower layers are usually older. Sedimentary rocks contain fossils, which tell us about the past, and they store energy resources such as coal, petroleum and natural gas.

Correction: the printed book says the bottom layer is always the hardest and the top the softest. Older layers are usually lower, but hardness does not depend on position. The book also numbers this section "2. Metamorphic rock"; it is the third type.

3. Metamorphic rock

Start-up questions
  • How are metamorphic rocks formed?
  • What is metamorphism? What causes it?

"Metamorphic" comes from the Greek meta (change) and morph (form). Metamorphic rocks are "changed rocks": they form when existing igneous, sedimentary or even metamorphic rocks are changed by heat and pressure inside the crust. Marble is an example: it forms from limestone.

FocusMetamorphism is the change of existing rocks into new rocks. Heat and pressure are its most important agents. Limestone → (heat and pressure) → marble.

Distribution and economic value of rock types in Ethiopia

Competency: Describe where the different rock types are found in Ethiopia and their economic value.
Start-up questions

A. Distribution of rocks

All three rock types are found in Ethiopia. Igneous rocks cover large areas in the central part of the country. Eastern Ethiopia is mainly covered by sedimentary rocks. Metamorphic rocks are found along the northern, western and southern edges.

B. Economic importance of rocks in Ethiopia

Rocks have many uses that make them important to people:

  1. Fuel: sedimentary rocks such as coal are a source of fuel for industry.
  2. Construction: granite and sandstone are quarried for roads, bridges and buildings. Marble is polished to decorate floors and walls.
  3. Food: rock salt (sodium chloride) from sedimentary rock is used in cooking.
  4. Soil: soils form when rocks slowly break down.
