Theorem 1.1 · Euclid's division lemma
Given a non-negative integer a and a positive integer b, there are unique non-negative integers q and r with
a = (b × q) + r,   0 ≤ r < b
a is the dividend, b the divisor, q the quotient and r the remainder.

Example 1. (a) 38 ÷ 4: 38 = 4 × 9 + 2. (b) 5 ÷ 14: 5 is less than 14, so 5 = 14 × 0 + 5; the quotient is 0 and the remainder is 5. (c) 12 ÷ 3: 12 = 3 × 4 + 0. (d) 2,574 ÷ 8 by long division: 2,574 = 8 × 321 + 6.

Notea is divisible by b when the remainder is 0. In (c), 12 is divisible by 3.

Definition 1.1 · Prime and composite numbers
A natural number with exactly two different factors, 1 and itself, is a prime number. A natural number with more than two factors is a composite number.

Prime factorization

Definition 1.2 · Prime factorization
Writing a number as a product of prime numbers is called prime factorization.

To factorize a composite number, split it into two factors. If a factor is still composite, split it again. Stop when every factor is prime.

Examples. 6 = 2 × 3. 30 = 2 × 15 and 15 = 3 × 5, so 30 = 2 × 3 × 5. For 72: 72 = 2 × 36, 36 = 2 × 18, 18 = 2 × 9, 9 = 3 × 3, so 72 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 = 2³ × 3². A factor tree shows these splits.

Example 5. 456 ÷ 2 = 228, 228 ÷ 2 = 114, 114 ÷ 2 = 57, 57 ÷ 3 = 19, and 19 is prime. So 456 = 2³ × 3 × 19.

Theorem 1.2 · Fundamental theorem of arithmetic
Every composite number can be written as a product of primes, and this factorization is unique (apart from the order of the factors).

Definition 1.3
(i) A number that is a factor of each of two or more natural numbers is a common factor. (ii) The largest common factor is the greatest common factor (GCF), also called the highest common factor (HCF). GCF(a, b) means the GCF of a and b.

Example 1. Factors of 36: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36. Factors of 56: 1, 2, 4, 7, 8, 14, 28, 56. The common factors are 1, 2, 4, so GCF(36, 56) = 4. Figure 1.1 shows this as a Venn diagram, with the common factors in the overlap.

Definition 1.4
The least common multiple of natural numbers a and b, written LCM(a, b), is the smallest number that is a multiple of both a and b.

Example 3. Multiples of 6: 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, … Multiples of 9: 9, 18, 27, 36, … The common multiples are 18, 36, 54, …, so LCM(6, 9) = 18.

Definition 1.5 · Rational numbers
A number that can be written as ab, where a and b are integers and b ≠ 0, is a rational number. The set of rational numbers is
ℚ = {a/b : a, b ∈ ℤ and b ≠ 0}

Example 1. 3/4, −13/10, −6 and 0 are rational. −6 = −6/1 and 0 = 0/1, so every integer is rational, and so is every natural number: ℕ ⊆ ℤ ⊆ ℚ.

Note: kinds of fractionsLet x = a/b be a fraction with a, b positive. (i) If a < b, x is a proper fraction. (ii) If a ≥ b, x is an improper fraction. (iii) A whole number with a proper fraction, such as 5¼, is a mixed fraction. (iv) x is in simplest form (lowest terms) when GCF(a, b) = 1, that is, a and b are relatively prime.

Note: the book states (i) and (ii) for any integers a and b. They are meant for positive numerators and denominators: by the book's rule, −5/2 would count as "proper" because −5 < 2.

Example 2. 7/8 and 2/9 are proper; 7/3 and 6 (= 6/1) are improper; 5¼ is mixed.

Example 3. To change a mixed fraction to an improper fraction: l m/n = l + m/n = (l × n + m)/n. So 3¼ = (3 × 4 + 1)/4 = 13/4.

Any rational number a/b can be written as a decimal by dividing a by b. One of two things happens:

  • The division stops with remainder 0. The decimal is a terminating decimal, such as 3/5 = 0.6.

1.3.1 Decimals that neither terminate nor repeat

Terminating and repeating decimals are rational. Now we meet decimals that do neither.

A perfect square is an integer times itself: 1 = 1², 4 = 2², 9 = 3², … A square root of x is a number y with y² = x. For example, 5 and −5 are square roots of 25, because 5² = (−5)² = 25.

A square with area 2 cm². How long is its side s? Since 1² = 1 and 2² = 4, s is between 1 and 2. Squeeze further: 1.4² < 2 < 1.5², so 1.4 < s < 1.5. Then 1.41 < s < 1.42, then 1.414 < s < 1.415, and so on. We write s = √2 = 1.414…. The sign √ is the radical sign.

Example 1. (a) √4 = 2, since 2² = 4. (b) √9 = √(3²) = 3 = 3/1. (c) −√9 = −3. (d) √0.0016 = √(0.04)² = 0.04 = 4/100 = 1/25.

Comparing real numbers

Each real number is one point on the number line, so any two real numbers can be compared.

  • Trichotomy: for real a and b, exactly one of a < b, a = b, a > b is true.
  • Transitive: if a < b and b < c, then a < c.
  • For non-negative a and b: if a² < b², then a < b.

Example 1. (a) −8 is left of −2, so −8 < −2. (b) 5/8 = 0.625 < 0.8. (c) √2/3 ≈ 0.4714 > 0.34. (d) Compare √3/6 and 2/3 by squaring: (√3/6)² = 3/36 and (2/3)² = 4/9 = 16/36. So √3/6 < 2/3.

Rounding whole numbers

First find the rounding digit: the tens digit when rounding to the nearest 10, the hundreds digit for the nearest 100, and so on. Then look at the digit to its right:

  • 0, 1, 2, 3 or 4: keep the rounding digit. All digits to its right become 0.
  • 5, 6, 7, 8 or 9: add 1 to the rounding digit. All digits to its right become 0.

Example 1. A town has 38,721 people. To the nearest 100 this is 38,700 (the digit after 7 is 2, so 7 stays). To the nearest 1,000 it is 39,000 (the digit after 8 is 7, so 8 becomes 9). To the nearest 10,000 it is 40,000. The rounded numbers are less accurate but easier to use.

Correction: the printed book gives 38,721 to the nearest 100 as "36,700" and calls it "rounded up". It is 38,700, and it is rounded down.

Decimal places

To round to a number of decimal places, look at the next digit. Example 2. (a) 10.673 to 1 d.p.: the second digit is 7 (5 or more), so round up: 10.7. (b) 3.173 to 2 d.p.: the third digit is 3, so 3.17. Rounding to 1 d.p. means to the nearest tenth; 2 d.p. means to the nearest hundredth.

Significant figures

Definition 1.14 · Lower and upper bound
The lower and upper bounds are the limits of the values a number could have had before it was rounded.

1.5, 1.50 and 1.500 look the same but are written to different accuracies. 1.5 (to 1 d.p.) could be anything from 1.45 up to, but not including, 1.55 (Figure 1.9):

1.45 ≤ x < 1.55

1.45 is the lower bound and 1.55 the upper bound. To find them:

  1. Find the place value of the accuracy (for example 1, or 0.1).
  2. Halve it.
  3. Subtract the half from the value for the lower bound; add it for the upper bound.

Example 1. A tree weighs 872 kg to the nearest kg. Half of 1 is 0.5, so the bounds are 871.5 kg and 872.5 kg: 871.5 ≤ w < 872.5.

Example 2. 10.7 to 1 d.p.: half of 0.1 is 0.05, so the bounds are 10.65 and 10.75.

Note: Definition 1.14 says the bounds are "the maximum and minimum values". Strictly, the upper bound itself is not allowed: 1.55 rounds to 1.6, not 1.5. That is why the inequality uses < on the right.

21. Positive integers a, b and c leave remainders 9, 7 and 10 when divided by 13. Show that a + b + c is divisible by 13.
a = 13p + 9, b = 13q + 7, c = 13r + 10. Then a + b + c = 13(p + q + r) + 26 = 13(p + q + r + 2), a multiple of 13.
